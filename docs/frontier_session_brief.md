## 1. Goal and the final decision

**The question.** Given a fixed budget to *make* training data for a sequence-to-function (S2F) model, what data should you make? Three axes: **reservoir** (how candidate sequences are generated), **acquisition** (which candidates are labelled), and **scale** (how many). The deliverable is guidance of the form "for budget B and target task T, generate and select sequences this way", not a model. The project frames reservoirs as "the most informative data source" rather than as active learning in the narrow sense, with acquisition as a second layer on top.

**The final decision.** Two sets of MPRA experiments with a synthesis budget of about **4M sequences each**: human (tested in **two cell lines, K562 and HepG2**) and yeast. I must propose which reservoir and acquisition strategies to test, in what proportions, and pre-register the analysis in a registered report. Depending on how many hypotheses and strategies the report contains, each reservoir/acquisition strategy may get **anywhere from 300k to 1M sequences**. In the registered report the starting point is the full base data (300k Gosai genomic sequences in human, 6M random promoters in yeast) with each strategy's sequences added on top, and the models will be the best available (Section 2). The in-silico work here, labelled by an oracle and run with proxy students, is the evidence base for that proposal.

**Cell-type specificity is a major application.** A key use of the resulting models is to predict sequences that are highly active in one cell line and inactive in the other (K562-high/HepG2-low and the reverse), not only to predict activity in each. Everything so far has been measured on K562 activity; extending the evaluations and the acquisition rules to the **differential** (specificity) objective is part of the plan, and I want advice on it (Sections 4, 6, 14).

**Standing methodological goal.** Find the simplest procedure that approximates ideal performance across reservoirs and dataset sizes, while marginalizing over factors such as architecture and training recipe so that the scaling results are general; a more complicated procedure must beat it by more than noise to earn its cost.

**Why it is not obvious.** A synthetic reservoir can beat real genomic sequence, because genomic sequence is a biased, functionally constrained sample of a narrow activity range. Which synthetic strategy wins, and whether that depends on scale or on the evaluation target, has not been studied systematically.

## 2. Background and data sources

**Human data.** Gosai et al. (2024) [1] trained their model on 776,474 sequences of 200 bp (about 798k unique oligos before filtering), assayed by an **episomal, plasmid-based MPRA** in K562, HepG2 and SK-N-SH, and then tested a separate library of 77,157 sequences: about 51,000 synthetic sequences designed with machine guidance (including motif-penalised designs), about 24,000 natural sequences (DNase-hypersensitive and model-selected) and 2,157 controls. Our genomic reservoir is the natural-sequence part of this library (about 315k sequences), so genomic cannot be scaled beyond that; the designed sequences supply our high-activity ("OOD") evaluation set. We work from a cleaned copy of the released data and use the K562 and HepG2 readouts. Paired episomal and integrated (lentiviral) HepG2 data from a different study [15] were used for one cross-check of the GC dependence (Section 12).

**The oracle.** Synthetic sequences have no measurements, so everything is labelled by an **oracle**: an AlphaGenome [2] model adapted to MPRA data following the encoder-only approach described on the Genomics x AI blog [3] (the AlphaGenome convolutional encoder, positional handling adapted for short inputs, pooled embeddings and a lightweight regression head; reported on that blog as state of the art on lentiMPRA, STARR-seq and CAGI5, and about 500 times cheaper to run than full AlphaGenome). We fine-tune it on the Gosai data as a chromosome-split 10-fold cross-validated ensemble (stamped `oracle_v3`, checked on every pool). Its held-out test Pearson against the experiment is about 0.93 (SD 0.005 across folds); it is lower out of distribution (about 0.84) and for single-nucleotide-variant effect sizes (about 0.39). All results below are Pearson r **against oracle labels** unless stated. Oracle-based benchmarking has known pitfalls [12], which is why Section 12 exists.

**Yeast data.** The Random Promoter DREAM Challenge [4] released **6,739,258 random 80 bp promoter sequences** (padded to 150 bp for modelling) with mean expression measured in yeast by cell sorting with a YFP reporter, plus a 71,103-sequence test set of several promoter types (random, native, designed high and low, SNV pairs, motif perturbations and tilings). The yeast oracle is a DREAM-RNN model: the challenge paper's Prix Fixe framework recombined the building blocks of the top three entries into **DREAM-CNN** (convolutional core), **DREAM-RNN** (bidirectional-LSTM core) and **DREAM-Attn** (transformer core). Related earlier work on random-promoter MPRA and models is [5]. The yeast readout is activity-binned sorting, qualitatively different from the barcode counts of the human data; measurements at the extremes are noisier and predictions are compressed relative to the measured range. The yeast plan is organised around **base datasets of 600k and 6M random promoters** (6M is essentially the full challenge-scale base, kept because the point is to test the power of selection on top of a large base), with additions from each strategy.

**Students: not one architecture.** We deliberately **do not fix a student architecture**. MPRA-LegNet [6] is a well-performing model on small to medium MPRA datasets and serves as a known-good reference, but the purpose of the experiment is to **marginalize architecture as a confounder** of the scaling results. We therefore generate and test many architectures: convolutional towers with different block types (squeeze-and-excite, inverted residual, dilated, multi-scale kernels), recurrent and gated models (bidirectional GRU, weight-tied recurrent dilated blocks), attention and transformer hybrids (a small transformer over motif tokens), MLP-mixer styles, U-Net styles, and explicit motif/grammar models, as well as the DREAM-style architectures for yeast, with free choices of optimizer (Adam, AdamW, Muon [14]), schedule, width and depth (up to 40M parameters). Performance is reported for **ensembles** of these students so that the number reflects what the data supports rather than what one architecture extracted.

**The students differ between the in-silico study and the registered report, by necessity.** In the registered report we want to test the best available models: an **ensemble of AlphaGenome-encoder-plus-head models** (the model class we currently use as the oracle ensemble) in human cells, and a **DREAM-RNN ensemble** in yeast. We cannot test those in silico: they are the oracles, and an in-silico student must not be nearly the same model as the oracle (the same architecture with the same massive pretraining would reproduce its own labels almost immediately and hide differences between strategies). So the in-silico students are from-scratch architectures, a **proxy** for the real students. How far conclusions from the proxy transfer to pretrained state-of-the-art students is an open validity question (Sections 12 to 14).

**Three regimes, and which matters most.** (a) *From scratch*: students trained only on the strategy's data, which is the scaling ladder. (b) *From a small base*: 30k Gosai genomic sequences (human) or 600k random promoters (yeast) plus the strategy's sequences. (c) *From the full base*: 300k Gosai genomic sequences (human) and 6M random promoters (yeast), which is the **starting point in the registered report**, with the library's sequences added on top. Regime (c) is arguably the most relevant to the registered report; (a) and (b) are informative about which regime each strategy helps in, and they are where our proxy students have the most headroom (the from-scratch menu ensemble reaches 0.863 at 300k against an oracle ceiling of about 0.93, whereas a state-of-the-art student started from the full base is already near the oracle's level and has little headroom). I do not know where to put the most emphasis, and would like advice (Section 14).

**AutoResearch (how architectures are generated).** An LLM-driven search: each round a language model (Claude Sonnet 5.5 in the main setup) proposes two candidates, each a complete PyTorch network plus a training recipe; each is trained and scored on **validation only** (audited after an earlier version leaked test scores to the proposer); the history is fed back. The prompt carries guidance (known-good blocks and a note that models will be ensembled). One search of about 20 to 60 models is a **run**; its best models by validation become candidates. Ensembling is a non-negative ElasticNet fitted on validation predictions of all candidates ("enet_all_pos"), which beat greedy top-K selection by about +0.01; uncertainty weighting and DPP diversity selection gave nothing.

**Pools.** A dataset is a labelled pool of sequences from one reservoir; datasets of increasing size are **nested prefixes** of a pool so a size comparison varies only the amount of data. Independent runs can each get an independent labelled pool ("replicate"). Early pools were not nested; this was audited and corrected (Section 8).

| Family | Strategies and their knobs | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Genomic (real sequence)** | *Gosai genomic CREs* (about 315k, all that exist, so it cannot scale and eventually plateaus); *ENCODE accessibility windows* (descoped: largely already in the Gosai library, only about 100k more, partition by cell type possible); *Zoonomia orthologs* [9] (real aligned orthologous CRE sequences from the 241-mammal alignment, capacity about 8.2M sequences; tiers by taxonomy: all mammals, one or two intermediate clades, primates only; being rebuilt by deleting unalignable N positions and extending with real flanking sequence rather than filling them; earlier arms contained fabricated filler); the older "mutate human CREs at conservation-derived rates" variant with a flat-rate control | Real sequence is functionally constrained and tests the data distribution the assay was built around; the Zoonomia rebuild is untried |
| **Genomic perturbation** | *EvoAug-style structural perturbations* [7] (probabilities of deletion, insertion, inversion, translocation, tandem duplication, point mutation; mutation rate; maximum indel size; base can be Gosai genomic or Zoonomia orthologs); *evoaug_paper2023* (the published settings, the control arm); *evoaug_ours_default* (untuned); *random mutagenesis* at an oracle-tuned rate (fixed, uniform or normal rate distribution; base genomic or Zoonomia) | Weaker or stronger perturbation of real sequence; mutagenesis applied also to motif strategies to obtain weak as well as strong sites |
| **Random and composition controls** | *uniform random*; *GC-matched random* (matches the genomic GC histogram); *dinucleotide shuffle* (keeps dinucleotide composition, destroys motif syntax); *random at fixed GC* (for example 0.65); *random GC sweep* (equal batches across GC 0.25 to 0.75, each synthesised as its own batch then pooled in any ratio, cost linear in the number of levels) | There is no reason to commit to a single GC content; the high-GC arm of random sequence manufactures spurious GC-box motifs the oracle rewards |
| **Motif-based (planted motifs)** | Vocabulary from JASPAR or from CIS-BP (3,301 motifs, clustered into PFM families so similar binding sites are not over-sampled); *shared core* (TFs profiled in both K562 and HepG2), *cell-type enriched* (profiled in one of the two; can be up-weighted, for example 20 percent from the target cell types and 80 percent from everything else), *syntax core* (a restricted vocabulary crossed combinatorially for interaction learning); number of motifs per sequence (3 to 7), density, sequence background (genomic or random), potency (PWM sampling vs consensus stamping); **planned or not yet parameterised**: motif order, spacing and orientation (placement currently needs sampler work) and mutagenesis of planted sites to obtain weak binding sites | The only family that reaches high activity cheaply; none of our current settings reaches the activity of the designed Gosai set |
| **Model-guided / generative** | *D3 discrete diffusion* [8] with the oracle as the utility function that tilts generation (conditioning on accessibility, on activity, or on ranges and distributions of activity, including accessible-and-repressive, not just activating); *in-silico evolution against the oracle* (single objective now; the differential K562-vs-HepG2 variant needs a multi-cell-type predictor); *GPN-STAR*; *Evo 2* [13] generation | Mostly **not yet run**; scalable and tunable toward the target distribution |
| **Mixtures** | Pairs, trios, uneven and within-family mixtures of the above (a 21-corpus sweep is running) | Cost of mixing is just ordering batches separately and pooling in any ratio |

## 4. The evaluation sets, the acquisition strategies, and the two-cell-line objective

**Evaluation sets (18 oracle-labelled sets used in the current battery).** All are scored against oracle labels of K562 activity; held-out chromosomes (test chr7 and chr13, validation chr19, chr21, chrX) never appear in any reservoir (audited, zero overlap).

| Set | How it is built | What it probes |
|---|---|---|
| `genomic` | Real held-out genomic sequences from the test chromosomes (n = 31,435) | In-distribution accuracy on natural sequence; also scored against experimental labels |
| `ood` | The high-activity **designed** Gosai sequences (mean oracle activity about 4.0 vs 0.3 to 1.1 for everything we generate) | Extrapolation to the activity range people design for |
| `random_32k` | 32k uniform random sequences | Behaviour on unconstrained sequence |
| `gc_sweep` | Random sequences with varying GC content | GC-dependence, including the high-GC extrapolation regime |
| `dinuc_shuffle` | Dinucleotide shuffles of held-out CREs | Composition without motif syntax |
| `sub_low/med/high`, `ins_low/med/high`, `del_low/med/high` | Held-out sequences with substitutions (1 to 50 percent), insertions and deletions (1 to 10 percent) at low, medium and high rates | Robustness to perturbation |
| `inversion`, `translocation` | Held-out sequences with segments inverted or moved | Structural perturbation (the EvoAug family's test-side twin) |
| `snv_ref`, `snv_alt` | Reference and alternate alleles of genomic SNVs (Hamming-1 pairs only); the effect is ref minus alt | Variant effect prediction; hard because effects are tiny and the experimental noise is large |

Beyond the 18, each reservoir family has a **test-side twin built on the held-out chromosomes** (motif-planted sequences on test backgrounds with novel motif combinations, GC-matched to the test distribution, Zoonomia orthologs of test-chromosome windows built by inverting the sampler's chromosome exclusion), so "matched" evaluation is possible. Evaluation sets I would like to add: **motif recombination** (a shuffle-and-recombine design), **species variability**, and **cell-type differential** sets (K562-high/HepG2-low and the reverse). For manageability I intend to whittle the final battery to about 4 to 6 sets chosen from the applications that matter (genomic held-out, designed high-activity, SNV effect, random/GC, differential), and to keep the test sets **separate**, expecting the same answer across them; if some strategies cater to certain test sets over others, that is how it is, and not something to adjudicate by a single score. **Validation sets should not reflect the evaluation set**, since users will not have the evaluation set; the shared validation set is real genomic chr19/21/X sequence, and varying it (a subset of new sequences, or reservoir-matched validation chromosomes) is a later additional axis.

**Acquisition strategies (selection of which candidates to label).** Six rules (each with registered variants and controls):

1. **Random** selection (the floor).
2. **Uncertainty**: ensemble or MC-dropout predictive uncertainty. Raw uncertainty correlates with activity, so I also run
3. **Activity-normalised uncertainty**: the uncertainty *excess* over the trend at that activity level (bin by predicted activity, standardise the residual), so selection is not just re-ranking by activity.
4. **Diversity in the model's embedding space** (k-means). Caveat: if the penultimate layer is linear in activity, this clusters by activity; I want to know what the metric actually captures (sequence diversity, mechanistic diversity, or activity).
5. **Uncertainty plus diversity**: **BADGE** [10] (uncertainty-weighted embeddings seeded by k-means++; we use an expected-gradient version because the pseudo-label gradient is exactly zero for a Gaussian head and would silently reduce BADGE to random; controls `badge_epistemic_only` and `badge_kmeanspp_only` attribute any gain to the fusion) and **BatchBALD** [11] (greedy batch mutual information; warns when the batch exceeds the posterior rank, since an M-member ensemble has rank at most M minus 1). Textbook **binned** versions on discretised activity are also registered (needed for yeast and for continuous MPRA data), and multi-cell-type variants: aggregate uncertainty (summed variance across cell types) and **differential uncertainty** (absolute log variance ratio between two cell types).
6. **Activity-stratified selection**: even coverage of the **2D grid of activity in both cell types**, optionally followed by selection on motif diversity and activity. This is a strong human-prior baseline that active learning must beat, and the natural baseline for the specificity objective.

**Acquisition in the real experiment is single-round batch selection** from a pool using a model trained on existing base data (the registered-report regime is **300k Gosai genomic sequences in human and 6M random promoters in yeast**; 30k and 600k are secondary regimes), with oracle labels simulating the measurements. Acquisition is evaluated on pools drawn from the reservoirs, and also on mixed pools. A reservoir that acquisition functions rarely pick should not be included; synthetic-only pools only make sense if the model should not have seen the genome.

## 5. Design guidance I have received (distilled)

- **Realism.** The study should be as realistic as what people would actually do, unless a strong reason justifies a baseline. People typically have 30k to 100k measured sequences, so the starting-data regimes (30k and 300k human, 600k and 6M yeast) matter. Not much reason to restrict acquisition pools to genomic sequence.
- **Which reservoirs to carry forward.** Test strategies in silico, and drop a reservoir that no acquisition function selects from. Prioritise by a limited time budget, highest priority first. A minimum viable first pass is one strategy per family (for acquisition: k-means diversity and MC-dropout uncertainty).
- **Evaluation hygiene.** Keep test sets separate. Genomic evaluation is vulnerable to information leakage (models memorise near-duplicates); excluding sequences by k-mer similarity is a poor idea because it removes legitimate motif spacings and orientations; local-alignment homology scores are the better tool. SNV effects are hard to evaluate because of tiny effects and experimental noise. Do not mix data from different experimental batches unless necessary; if old and new measurements must be combined, anchor sequences spanning the expression range allow a linear transformation between batches (imperfect, but adequate).
- **Yeast is different.** Activity-binned sorting, noisier extremes, compressed predictions; keep the evaluation set as its own fold; model accuracy falls at the ends of the range.
- **Saturation.** The Gosai data are relatively saturated: an ensemble of AlphaGenome-based models is already near 0.9 Pearson, so with enough genomic data a student barely differs from the oracle and strategy differences wash out. One should find where saturation occurs and compare strategy rankings when starting from a small subset (for example 30k, or a small pretrained model with room to improve) against starting from the full set. Starting from scratch with too little data removes selection effects because the model knows too little; a model trained only on uncertain sequences does worse than one trained on random sequences because they are harder to learn.
- **Uncertainty and diversity.** Diversity alone is not a good criterion; diversity plus uncertainty is likely better. Uncertainty should be examined on **differential** activities. Baseline uncertainty scales with activity, so normalise and look for the excess. Inspect attribution maps of the sequences each strategy selects to see whether selection is mechanistically diverse or only sequence-diverse; where selection methods overlap strongly, drop redundant ones. Continuous MPRA data can be binned to make BADGE and BatchBALD easier to apply.
- **Target distributions.** Do not condition only on high activity: condition on ranges or distributions, including accessible-and-repressive. Few sequences in the library are cell-type-specific, so biasing the pool toward the target distribution helps even if the model chooses freely; narrowing the target pool may help active learning relative to giving it everything.
- **Motifs.** Use a comprehensive vocabulary (it is fine if some motifs are inactive), cluster similar binding sites and sample from clusters, place motifs randomly, compare uniform sampling with sampling biased to the target cell types (by up-weighting rather than restricting), and mutate motifs to include weaker sites. Planting and shuffling motifs may not give the mechanistic diversity needed because many mechanisms are shared across cell types.
- **Random-sequence reservoirs.** There is no reason to commit to a single GC content; order batches at several GC contents separately and pool in any ratio (cost is linear in the number of configurations).
- **Hyperparameters.** HP tuning matters mainly when the amount of data changes, perhaps not when only the reservoir changes; identify which HPs vary across sizes and reservoirs, and do not spend more effort on this than the goal warrants. Every protocol choice should be backed by data (one HP strategy vs two at half budget, proposals per round, rounds, total models, whether the optimal strategy transfers across reservoirs). Reach a good ensemble quickly per data point so that HP search does not dominate at 1M and above, and record what is learned so it does not have to be redone at each scale. Report on the 1 minus Pearson r and MSE scales for linearity.
- **Randomness.** Pinned random seeds can hide a seed artifact; use fresh randomness per replicate, and separate initialization variance from data-generation variance.
- **Rounds.** In this setting (a large array, few rounds), big rounds and few of them are attractive; in other settings a big first round would be wasteful. The process of the acquisition loop itself is still undefined and needs to be developed in parallel.
- **Software.** Keep the codebase simple, modular and easy to extend.

## 7. Methodology: the procedure under test, and the experiments

**The reusable per-size ensemble (why there is no hyperparameter search at every dataset size).** From a few independent AutoResearch runs on genomic data we keep a *menu* of about 30 architectures together with their training recipes (optimizer, learning rate, weight decay, batch size, schedule, epoch cap). The menu is frozen. For every reservoir (or mixture) and every dataset size, the whole menu is retrained with those recipes (early stopping on the validation set, size-scaled time caps), three seeds each, and combined by a non-negative ElasticNet fitted on validation predictions; the trained models, their predictions on every evaluation set and the ensemble weights are stored. Consequences: (i) a new reservoir, mixture or dataset size costs one retraining of the menu rather than a new search, which is what makes testing many arms and **scaling past 100k and 300k in silico** feasible (the in-silico setting can generate and test as many sequences as we like); (ii) architecture is marginalized because the ensemble spans about 30 architectures from several independent searches (all run on genomic data, so whether genomic-derived architectures favour genomic data is itself under test, experiments (a) and (c) below); (iii) the evidence that this is enough: bespoke per-reservoir searches match the retrained menu within noise at 30k and 100k, and architecture rankings at one size are noise-limited, so selecting architectures per size has no demonstrated value. What is **not** validated: that fixed recipes stay adequate at 1M and above (training length and learning rates may need to change) and, more generally, hyperparameter transfer across scale. So at the largest sizes I plan to retrain a reduced menu with the recorded recipes and spot-check it against a fresh search at one size.

**The simplest procedure under test.** (1) Run AutoResearch on genomic data: three independent runs, each on its own pool, about 20 to 30 models per run, keep the top 3 to 5 per run; this gives a menu of about 30 architectures. (2) For every reservoir and size, retrain the whole menu (three seeds) and ensemble with the non-negative ElasticNet. (3) Compare reservoirs and mixtures per size, fit scaling curves, extrapolate.

## 8. Evaluation details and controls

- One loop for all models, reverse-complement-averaged predictions, validation-only feedback to the proposer, validation early stopping, GPU-scaled time caps, a screen removing models with absurd predictions on any evaluation set (0.8 percent of 2,040 audited models exploded on some shifted set while validating normally).
- Same evaluation sets everywhere; the metric is Pearson r with oracle labels. Procedure decisions (search design, ensembling, menu) are made on genomic held-out sequences with the OOD and random sets as secondary checks, and SNV effect-size correlation is kept separate; strategies themselves are compared set by set, not by one combined score (Section 4).
- Equal-count controls when comparing arms: matched numbers of candidate architectures (top 3 per run) and trained models.
- Nested pools within a replicate, independent pools across runs (synthesised strategies get independent generation seeds, measured overlap 0 percent; genomic replicates are permutations of the 315k real elements so they share about 95 percent of sequences at 300k).
- Retest ceilings for ranking claims: how well one retraining of an architecture predicts the mean of its other retrainings at the same size (Spearman 0.50, 0.55, 0.61, 0.71 at 10k, 30k, 100k, 300k).
- Noise floor for "no improvement": about 0.005 in ensemble r (between-run SD of a pooled ensemble 0.002 to 0.005; single-run SD about 0.009; replicate-ensemble SD on one pool 0.0003 to 0.0015, which is why a shared pool understates variance).
- Seeds: every independent run gets its own generation seed (a distinct independent draw per replicate), and initialization variance is separated from data-generation variance.
- Provenance stamps on every pool and result; legacy per-size pools are flagged and refused by the loader on request.

## 10. Hypotheses: deliberately few

I want to keep the pre-registered hypotheses light, because most hypotheses will be generated in response to the pilot experiments rather than before them. Two are stated now:

- **S (scaling).** Error scales log-linearly with dataset size, both for 1 minus Pearson r and for MSE (a power law in D), and based on the results so far this will continue up to 1M and beyond.
- **G (genomic).** Genomic data (specifically the Gosai sequences; Zoonomia is untried) will be among the better strategies, but it will run out of examples and eventually plateau, and would plateau even if more examples existed because genomic sequences are functionally constrained.

Everything else is a **working expectation, not a hypothesis to register**: that motif-planted and augmentation arms do well at small sizes, that random and GC-matched plateau, that acquisition gains over random selection are small at a fixed pool and budget and larger on a heterogeneous pool, that a single round loses little relative to three, and that architecture and the proposer change absolute levels but not rankings. I would like advice on how to pre-register in a way that honours this (confirmatory hypotheses few, exploratory analyses declared, and a pre-specified rule for generating hypotheses from the pilot).

## 11. Draft statistical framework for the registered report (proposals to attack)

**Two layers.** (I) The in-silico, oracle-labelled tournament that selects arms (P1 to P6); its analysis is documented as design justification and the pilot results generate the hypotheses. (II) The wet-lab experiment whose analysis plan is pre-registered. I have not fixed how much of (I) belongs in the report.

**Units and estimands.** Student ensembles trained on subsets of each arm's data; the estimand is the difference in ensemble Pearson r on pre-specified held-out sets between two arms at matched sequence budget, and the difference in fitted scaling parameters. The experimental unit is the independent draw (pool replicate) of an arm, not the sequence, so the effective sample size is small (3 to 15). In the wet-lab experiment independent replicates of a library arm would consume budget, so resampling (bootstrap over sequences and over independent training subsets) may have to stand in.

**Variance components.** Data draw, initialization, architecture (marginalized), search run. I propose a mixed-effects model with crossed random effects for pool replicate and architecture, or a hierarchical Bayesian version, with arm as a fixed effect and size entering through a power-law mean function.

**Margins.** An equivalence and superiority margin delta from the replicate noise (about 0.005) and practical importance (what change in r changes the library design?). I do not know how to defend the choice.

**Multiplicity.** Dozens of arms times several evaluation sets times several sizes times two cell lines. Options: hierarchical gatekeeping, Holm within families, or Bayesian shrinkage with a region of practical equivalence.

**Design alternatives for reservoirs and acquisition.** (A) Sequential: reservoirs alone at random acquisition (P1), then acquisition on the winning pool (P3), then a joint check (P4). Cheap but misses interactions. (B) A fractional factorial over reservoir x acquisition x size from the start. More informative, expensive. (C) Adaptive racing: run all arms at a small size, drop dominated arms, spend the budget on survivors, with pre-registered futility and efficacy rules. My leaning is (C) layered on (A) with (B) only for finalists, but I am unsure whether racing is compatible with a clean registered-report analysis.

**Acquisition specifics.** The real experiment is single-round, so the comparison must be paired (same pool, same budget, random selection as control, and 2D activity-stratified selection as the strong baseline), the selection model must not have seen the evaluation chromosomes, and every result must carry its controls and a check that the selected batch differs from a random one. For the single-vs-three-round comparison (P3b), a pre-specified acceptable loss in r is needed.

**Power.** Simulate from the observed variance components; the quantities that matter are the number of independent pools per arm and the number of seeds, not the number of sequences.

## 12. Does the oracle landscape generalize to the experiment? (what I know, what worries me, candidate checks)

**What we know.** The oracle agrees with experiment at about 0.93 on held-out genomic sequences, about 0.84 OOD and about 0.39 on SNV effect sizes. Internal findings that bear on this: (i) the GC/CpG dependence of activity has real support but is much weaker in experiment than in our models. Paired episomal and integrated HepG2 data [15] show a GC U-shape in both assays, but it is weak and marginal episomally (the assay Gosai used) and about three times stronger when integrated, with effects of only about 0.1 to 0.27 log2 units across the GC range, far smaller than our models predict for random DNA; so the direction is biology, while the models' strong U-shape and high-GC runaway are largely extrapolation beyond the experimental ground truth. (ii) A published random-sequence episomal STARR-seq dataset in HepG2 [16] (a different geometry, not K562) agrees with models trained on the Gosai data: correlations of +0.51 (LegNet) and +0.54 (Malinois) between prediction and measurement on random enhancers, and motif-level GC dependence of +0.36 and +0.40 against +0.40 in experiment; the failures are compositional, at extreme GC and in the CpG richness of uniform random sequence (the models read CpG as activity, and genomic sequence is CpG-depleted). (iii) High-GC random-sequence activity is driven by pseudo-GC-box motifs (GC-box core hit rate 0.166 in high-GC random sequence against 0.006 to 0.008 in mid-GC and genomic sequence, about 20 times), so random sequence can look good partly because it manufactures spurious motifs the oracle rewards; the oracle's fold-to-fold disagreement is a clean extrapolation detector (correlation 0.93 with error, specific to high GC) and could be used to weight or reject synthetic labels; AlphaGenome-based models predict designed sequences well (probably because models share the same known motifs) but struggle with variant effects, which indicates patterns that are fundamentally missing.

**What worries me.** (1) A reservoir could win only by exploiting oracle idiosyncrasies in regions without training data (random and motif-planted sequence are exactly there). (2) Students learn the oracle's function, so a reservoir matching the oracle's training distribution is advantaged regardless of biology. (3) Models trained and evaluated on oracle labels cannot detect shared errors, and benchmarks that use the same model class for design and evaluation are known to be overconfident [12]. (4) The evaluation sets for synthetic sequences are oracle-labelled by construction. (5) The oracle is nearly saturated on Gosai genomic data (ensemble near 0.9), so strategy differences may wash out at large genomic sizes in silico yet matter in the experiment.

**Candidate checks (cost-ordered, my ideas).** Cross-label with a second oracle of a different architecture and see whether rankings survive; weight or filter synthetic labels by oracle disagreement and see whether rankings change; evaluate students on real measured data wherever it exists (genomic held-out; published random-sequence and variant datasets; designed sets) and compare rankings with oracle-labelled rankings; test oracle ensembles trained on genomic alone vs genomic plus high-activity designed sequences to see whether the latter helps or hurts; a small real MPRA pilot of a few thousand sequences from each of the top arms. The registered report could make the pilot's rank agreement with the oracle a pre-specified gate.

## 13. Weaknesses and sources of uncertainty (what makes me uneasy)

1. **Everything is against oracle labels**, and only K562 so far; HepG2 and the differential objective are untested. No synthetic reservoir has been validated against real measurements.
2. **Extrapolation.** The largest tested size is 300k (1M planned for finalists) and the libraries are 4M. In-distribution gains per doubling are falling (0.051, 0.028, 0.023) but OOD gains per doubling are not falling (about 0.07, 0.06 and 0.10 per doubling at 10k to 30k, 30k to 100k and 100k to 300k); orderings could change in between. Genomic cannot be scaled past about 300k.
3. **The search and the menu were optimised for few strategies.** The menu comes from searches on genomic data (and some comparisons used one representative per family: five reservoirs studied in depth, six more in progress), so strategies it was not optimised for might be handicapped, and families are broader than their representatives. Several important arms (Zoonomia rebuild, D3, in-silico evolution, GC-sweep random, motif order/spacing variants) have not been run in the new setup. The planned test is three independent searches on non-optimised strategies and on genomic, across dataset sizes, asking whether the genomic-versus-other gap under the fixed menu differs from the gap when every strategy is searched separately (Section 7 (c); preliminary numbers in Section 9: no evidence of a bias, within noise). It is launched but not finished (six unseen reservoirs at 30k and 10k, three at 100k), and I want your view on how to power and interpret it (Section 14).
4. **LLM search variance and reproducibility.** Single-run SD about 0.009. The proposer is a hosted model I can neither pin nor seed: the command-line interface gives no temperature or seed control, hosted model versions are updated and retired over time, and the prompt and guidance changed between experiments (guidance alone moved the score by +0.02). So searches run weeks or months apart are not strictly exchangeable and a search cannot be re-run exactly. Different proposers also differ (Opus 5.5 +0.005 and Sonnet 4.5 -0.005 against Sonnet 5.5, each under 2 standard errors at 50 models). Mitigation: the frozen menu (architectures and recipes), not the search, is the reproducible artefact and would be archived with the registered report; I will check the recorded settings of the current program's runs for exchangeability.
5. **Architecture marginalization is a sample**: about 30 to 45 architectures from one proposer family; very large attention models and pretrained backbones are not sampled.
6. **Ranking noise.** Architecture ranks are noise-limited at every size, so "this architecture is best at this size" claims are unsupported.
7. **Pool structure.** Genomic replicates overlap by 95 percent at 300k; early pools were not nested; and duplicate rates of the current synthetic pools should be re-measured before any final run (an earlier sampler returned 34 percent duplicate sequences until it was fixed and audited).
8. **Multiple comparisons** and a noise floor (0.005) chosen by judgement, with n = 3 to 4 runs in several comparisons.
9. **Acquisition has not been studied in the new setup**, its known failure modes are silent, and the acquisition loop itself (single vs multiple rounds, starting data, pool structure) is not yet defined.
10. **The mixture design space is huge**; model-level mixing is not the same as training on a mixed corpus.
11. **Evaluation battery bias**: the shared genomic validation set may favour genomic-like reservoirs; synthetic evaluation sets flatter broad-coverage reservoirs; the designed high-activity set is not reached by any current reservoir.
12. **Proxy students.** The registered report will test pretrained state-of-the-art ensembles (AlphaGenome-encoder-plus-head, DREAM-RNN) that cannot be tested in silico because they are the oracles, so every in-silico conclusion rests on from-scratch proxy students. The proxies have more headroom than the real students in the registered-report regime (full base), so effects could be larger in silico than in the experiment.
13. **Compute limits** forced choices (20-model runs, three runs per reservoir, 30k for most arms) that may not carry to 100k and above; yeast has a different cost structure and oracle quality.
